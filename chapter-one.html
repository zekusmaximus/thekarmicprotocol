The Karmic Protocol

Chapter 1 Maya / Simulation

“Māyā is the illusion that veils the true nature of reality; by seeing through māyā, one awakens to ultimate truth.”

“A simulation creates a controlled illusion of reality, helping us test and understand complex systems without direct exposure to the real world.”

The convoy of black SUVs cut through pre-dawn fog, headlights carving tunnels through gray. Agent Fiona Gile sat in the rear vehicle, running the operational brief one final time: suspected manufacturing facility for devices potentially banned under three international treaties, high probability of evidence destruction, no expected armed resistance but prepare anyway.

The brief had promised a clean seizure. No armed resistance, fast warrants, seal the line. Virtual reality headsets promising nirvana, that could quiet the wanting in a person until what was left looked a lot like peace. That’s what scared them.

Her thumb brushed the safety, a twenty-year habit that had saved her twice.

The facility’s front gate stood open at 7:47 AM. Gile’s gut tightened.

“Any vehicles enter before we arrived?”

“Box truck to loading dock, forty minutes ago,” her partner said through comms. “Three occupants. No exit recorded.”

Forty minutes. Enough time to move inventory, sanitize records, position any number of surprises. Gile caught her partner’s reflection in the rearview mirror—jaw set, shoulders tight. He felt it too.

The radio crackled: “High-value target may be present. Key developer possibly on site.”

Gile acknowledged and keyed her mic. “Rodriguez, loading dock. Eyes on that truck before we breach.”

The SUVs rolled through the open gate. The facility looked newer than satellite imagery suggested—fresh paint, manicured landscaping, security cameras tracking their approach. Too polished for a place with something to hide.

Morning chill bit through her jacket as she stepped out. Dawn hadn’t broken yet, leaving everything in that gray, unreal space where objects existed without shadows. She withdrew the warrant, its weight heavier than it should have been.

Agents flowed into position. Not guns-out—intelligence suggested no resistance—but careful, practiced, ready.

“Let’s move,” she ordered.

The glass doors opened before she reached them.

A receptionist blinked, perhaps startled, perhaps rehearsed. The lobby was already lit bright as noon. Doors deeper inside stood propped open.

“Federal agents. We have a warrant to search these premises.” Gile held the papers, cataloging everything: visitor logs, live camera feeds, a desk too neat, hallway doors positioned for perfect visibility.

Someone had prepared this facility for inspection. Not to hide evidence but to showcase its absence.

Agents streamed past with evidence collection equipment. Gile signaled her partner to take the administrative offices. She approached the double doors leading to the main floor.

The raid at the retreat center a few weeks ago had been the same: clean rooms, five prototypes, and witnesses who explained everything. That’s where she’d first seen it: the Karmic Protocol, supposed to induce “enlightenment,” whatever the reports avoided defining.

Gile pushed through the double doors—

And stopped.

The factory floor sprawled before her, spotless and brightly lit. Assembly stations in perfect rows. Workers producing brightly colored tablets for children, complete with cartoon animal interfaces.

Not neural interfaces. Not consciousness-altering technology.

Children’s tablets.

And at the center of the room, surrounded by cameras and well-dressed adults and a dozen children on a field trip, stood Georgia Sato.

The CEO smiled, positioned perfectly for cameras that had already been broadcasting before federal agents even entered the building.

Her partner’s voice hit comms: “Boss, that box truck? School bus. Says ‘Future Innovators Field Trip.’”

Every camera swiveled toward her.

“I am Agent Fiona Gile with Homeland Security,” she announced, voice steady despite the cold rush in her chest. “We have a warrant to search these premises for illegal neural interface devices.”

A suited man stepped forward. “Agent Gile, perhaps you’d like to explain to these children why federal officers are disrupting their educational tour?”

Little faces stared at her. One girl clutched her demonstration tablet, tears starting to form.

“Please cooperate with the agents,” she managed. “This is a lawful search. We’ll complete our work as quickly as possible.”

Even as she spoke, Gile knew they would find nothing.

Georgia Sato’s voice carried that perfect pitch for broadcast. “We manufacture educational devices for children. Nothing more.”

The emphasis on children landed like a blade.

A young woman approached—mid-twenties, professional bearing—and handed Gile a business card. “Agent Gile, please forward a copy of that warrant to Senator Yost’s office at your earliest convenience.”

Jessica Everett, the card read. State Senate staff.

Gile took the card, but something made her look twice. The woman’s composure wasn’t civilian nervousness or political posturing. It was something else. The way she held herself, the way her eyes tracked the federal agents with tactical precision rather than alarm. Not fear. Assessment.

Gile filed the observation and turned back to the operation.

“Cameras off,” she said.

“We’re broadcasting live,” Georgia replied smoothly. “Transparency is important.”

Serials scanned. Manifests mirrored inventory. Server pulls matched licenses.

“Standard tablet architecture,” Rodriguez said.

“Educational software, parental controls,” another agent confirmed.

Each clean result tightened the knot in her stomach. The intelligence had been specific about neural interface production. This facility was supposed to be ground zero.

The lawyers weren’t just fast, they were coordinated. Business cards appeared in sequence: corporate defense, antitrust, securities litigation, media rights. Not scrambling. Executing a prepared plan.

Hours passed. A facility too legitimate, too ready.

Three hours later, they walked out empty-handed.

“That was a disaster,” her partner said in the SUV.

She didn’t disagree.

He turned his phone toward her. “Advent Industries just filed acquisition notices on three Sato subsidiaries. Timestamp hit the minute we breached.”

The pieces locked together.

Frank O’Hearn. Always framed as a concerned business leader reporting threats, never as someone who might weaponize federal law enforcement for profit.

“We weren’t law enforcement this morning,” she said quietly. “We were leverage.”

“Above our pay grade,” her partner muttered.

Gile watched the facility recede through the window. A legitimate business that would soon belong to Frank O’Hearn, courtesy of a federal raid that found nothing illegal but damaged everything anyway.

Cray called. “Tell me you found something.”

“We found children,” she said. “Sato knew we were coming.”

“That’s a serious accusation, Agent Gile.”

“It’s a serious situation. Advent Industries moved the moment we entered. O’Hearn used our investigation as cover for a hostile takeover.”

She heard Cray processing, recalibrating. “O’Hearn’s filings aren’t our concern.”

“Our concern just did his work for him.”

“Careful, Agent Gile.” The warning carried weight. “We executed a lawful warrant based on credible intelligence.”

“Where did the intelligence come from?”

The question hung between them.

“We’ll discuss this in person,” Cray said finally. “Ten minutes. And Gile, bring nothing in writing.”

The line went dead.

The SUV rolled through morning traffic. Gile opened a new document: Clean operation. No illegal devices. Children present during execution. Lawyers pre-positioned. Acquisition moves timed to breach.

Her partner leaned over. “I’ll back your report.”

“Think Cray knew?” he asked quietly as they approached headquarters.

Gile thought about her superior’s intensity during the briefing, his singular focus on the protocol threat. She thought about how easily conviction could be exploited, how genuine fear made people useful to those with different agendas.

“I think his fear made him useful,” she said.

They stepped into the elevator.

The raid was spotless: children, cameras, and a legal factory someone needed raided anyway.

But something was still happening. The retreat center prototypes had been real. The reports about people changing, becoming calmer, disengaging from ordinary ambition, those hadn’t been fabricated.

And if O’Hearn wanted Sato’s technology badly enough to weaponize federal law enforcement, that meant he thought it was worth something.

Which meant someone, somewhere, was still building it.

Gile saved her preliminary notes.

She’d spent twenty years believing the badge meant something. That warrants came from evidence, not agendas. That federal work served justice, not power.

Whatever came next, she’d document it exactly as it happened.

She’d tell the truth, especially if someone didn’t want her to.
